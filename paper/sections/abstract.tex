\begin{abstract}
Small open-weight language models are attractive as decision-makers because they are cheap, fast and can run on hardware their users already own. Yet the same weights can be prompted, scored, probed or fine-tuned into a policy, and how this choice interacts with scale has not been measured under controlled conditions. We introduce \deadeye{}, a benchmark and open harness for this measurement. It has seven seeded decision tasks with known oracles (a multi-armed bandit, a contextual bandit, loan approval under distribution shift, gridworld navigation, tic-tac-toe, blackjack and customer-support triage under a written policy) and six conversion methods: the assistant's own free reply as the unconverted reference, free-form generation, exact likelihood scoring of legal actions, a linear probe on hidden states, LoRA behavior cloning, and a probe on raw task features that uses no language model. Returns are normalized so that a random policy scores 0 and the oracle 1, and common random numbers pair every comparison. Every measurement was made on a single laptop, an Apple M2 Max with 32~GB of memory, so the study can be repeated end to end without a cluster or a cloud account. On four within-family ladders from 135M to 8B parameters, 28 base and instruct checkpoints, we test seven pre-registered hypotheses with every conversion method the machine can run. Five ablation blocks vary prompting, the reasoning budget, quantization, the amount of adaptation data and the structure of the task, and an exploratory comparison sets our conversions against purpose-built decision models that run on the same laptop. Under generation, normalized score changes by \result{[slope $b$, 95\% CI]} per tenfold increase in parameters. Fine-tuned with LoRA, the smallest rung of a ladder matches its largest rung, prompted zero-shot and 12 to 15 times larger, on \result{[k]} of seven tasks, \result{[but not / and also]} under shift, and likelihood scoring closes \result{[Z]}\% of the gap between the smallest and largest models. Code, configurations and per-decision logs are public.
\end{abstract}
